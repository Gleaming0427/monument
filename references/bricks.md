# Bricks

A brick is a generator in `kit/builder.js` (object `generators`) that turns one element of a monument file into many small solid pieces. Reuse a brick only when it renders the requested building faithfully; otherwise write a new one (below).

Units are metres. `x` points east, `z` points south, `y` up; the ground (the studio floor) is at `y = 0`. `rot` turns an element around the vertical axis, in radians.

## Round pieces, columns and photo reliefs

Pieces are not only boxes. `k.turned(shape, …)` lays a turned piece in the box's unit (`drum`, `shaft`, `flare`, `cone`, `torus`, `ball`, `bell`, `octagon`, `spike`), `k.lying` lays one along an axis, and `k.column(kind, [x, y, z], {height, diameter, order, drum, taper, out}, color, phase)` stands a whole column: base, shaft laid drum by drum with its entasis, and the capital of its order (`tuscan`, `doric`, `ionic`, `corinthian`, `romanesque`, `gothic`). Use them wherever the building is round: a column is never a square post, a pier with a round plan is never a box. The Gothic toolkit's `column` and `statue`, the amphitheatre's `order` per storey and the campanile's colonnades already do.

Carving that geometry cannot render (figures, foliage, tympana, friezes) is a `relief` element: a photograph rectified onto the model. It must be a free photo with its credit, and it is documented evidence like any other.
1. Find a frontal photo on Wikimedia Commons: perspective-corrected architecture photos and orthographic panoramas are best; a free licence is required (CC0, public domain, CC BY, CC BY-SA, Licence Ouverte).
2. `node scripts/relief.mjs monuments/<id>.json --file "File:<name>.jpg" --name <slug> [--crop x0,y0,x1,y1]` checks the licence, writes the image into `<id>.evidence/reliefs/` and prints its `image` and `credit`, with a grid to read pixels from. An owner's own photo (a survey, an orthophoto, a commissioned shot) goes in with `--image <photo.jpg> --author "…" --license "…"`: it serves a model its owner keeps, while the public gallery still needs a free licence.
3. Read the four corners of the carved panel on the grid and measure it on the model's scale: a known width in the photo (between buttresses, columns, the panel's own measured size) gives the pixels per metre.
4. `node scripts/relief.mjs monuments/<id>.json --check <element index>` draws the panel as the viewer lays it, with its outline in red: fix the corners and the shape until the carving sits inside it.
5. On the brick that carries it, leave room for it: `kings.relief`, a portal's `relief` (with its `inner` doorway, `doors`, `depth`, `foot`), a rose's `relief` keep only the opening and the walls around.

## Closed shells, roof frames and interiors

A model is a closed shell: whatever angle the camera takes, no wall stops short of a roof, no roof ends open, no opening shows the void inside. Check it from every side and from above, then from inside with the section view (`?section`, or the Section button: everything between the camera and the view's centre is cut away).
- Where runs meet (a nave and a transept, an apse and a choir), cut the walls of one where the other passes (`open`), carry the roofs on until they meet in valleys (`roof.extend`), and the vaults under a tower bay (`vault.extend`).
- An opening filled by a photo relief (a rose, a portal) keeps glass or its doors behind the photo, so that it is closed seen from inside.
- Vaults go under every roof of a church that has them, laid after the roof as they were: rib vaults whose wall arches rise over the clerestory windows, over the aisles and chapels too, and an arcade on round piers with the tribune's openings, so that the section shows the real elevation.

Roof frames are built in their phase, before the roof that covers them, and shown in the section view. Choose the `system` from the frames of the country and the century, and cite what was read (a frame survey, a dendrochronology study, the restoration's own pages):
- `sans-entrait`: the early Gothic frame of northern France (12th century): rafters held by collars and braces, no tie beams;
- `chevrons-formant-fermes`: Gothic France from about 1220: every rafter pair is a truss (collars, struts on sole pieces) and every `main` metres a principal truss adds a tie beam, a king post and braces, with ties along the roof joining the king posts;
- `fermes-pannes-chevrons` (`king-post`): from the late Middle Ages in England and generally from the 18th–19th centuries: king-post trusses carry purlins, and common rafters lie on them.
A frame burnt or replaced takes `until`, and its rebuilding `rebuild` with the fresh wood's `rebuildColor`.

Interior works that marked the building's history (an altar consecrated, a choir screen carved, an organ, a statue) are `furnishing` elements, and their carving a `relief` facing into the building. Give them a moment with `inside: true` and `look` [azimuth, elevation]: while it shows, the camera swings round to that side and the section view opens on them (see format.md).

## Change over time

Everything that happens to a building is tied to a phase:
- raise a building: a second element with a higher `y0`;
- ruin then restore: `"ruin": {"phase", "share", "restore"}`;
- take a whole element down: `until`; for `wing`, `roof.until` removes only the roof before a new storey goes on.
- lean a building as the ground gives way: `"lean": {"pivot": [x, y, z], "toward": [dx, 0, dz], "track": [[t, degrees], …]}` on an element; `t` is the timeline position in phases, and pins within `radius` lean with it. One leaning group per monument.

## Adding a brick

Write a generator `name(k, e, P)` modelled on the existing ones:
- `k.box`, `k.beam`, `k.oriented`, `k.turned`, `k.lying` or `k.column` for every piece, with its colour from `k.tint`, and its kind: `stone`, `wood`, `slate`, `tile`, `plaster`, `shingle`, `gilt` (gold leaf, mirroring the studio), `bark`, `metal`, `lead`, `glass` or `cloth`, each dressed with the monument's surface for it (visual-style.md);
- `P(e.phase)` for its phase;
- the `order` argument sets the laying order inside the phase: bottom to top by default, or out from the towers, as for the bridge.

Name it after the architecture (`ribbedVault`, `onionDome`, `setbackTower`), never after one building, and never branch on a building's id.

Where it goes:
- **In this repository** (`kit/builder.js`), add a row to the table above. `node scripts/validate.mjs` fails if a brick is missing from it.
- **In a user's project**, write `monuments/<id>.bricks.js` and set `"bricks": "<id>.bricks.js"` in the monument file. The module's default export is an object of generators. It may `import * as T from 'three'`, and `import {generators, gothic} from '../kit/builder.js'` to call core bricks such as `generators.facade`, or to use the Gothic toolkit (`gothic(k, e, P)`: walls, gables, arches, roofs, balustrades, pinnacles, statues and columns; with `"masonry": "mesh"` in the monument file its walls, arches, windows, roofs and balustrades are real surfaces, and `G.mesh` offers the rest: `shaft`, `spirelet`, `loft`, `pier`, `flyer`, `string`, `extrude`, `gargoyle`: a carved beast or a stone gutter built of silhouettes). The viewer loads it before building the scene, and `scripts/validate.mjs` reads its brick names. Import the kit with a literal relative specifier (`'../kit/builder.js'`): the site build stamps the deploy's version on it (`?v=…`) so that a visitor's cached engine and a newer bricks file are never mixed, and a specifier it cannot read is left unstamped (`scripts/deploy.test.mjs` holds it). Bricks sent to the community gallery are reviewed before they join the kit.

Bricks still to write, as requests come: setback skyscrapers (Empire State Building), domes, minarets, rock and terrain (Mont-Saint-Michel), stepped pyramids. These are reasons to build, never reasons to refuse.
